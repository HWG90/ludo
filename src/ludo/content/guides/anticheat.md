# Anti-cheat: the honest list

Single-player and most live-service games are a ProtonDB lookup. Competitive shooters with **kernel anti-cheat** are a publisher permission problem. Proton cannot bypass a vendor that refuses to load on Linux.

## The landscape

- **Easy Anti-Cheat (EAC)** and **BattlEye** can work on Linux when the *game* enables the Linux/Proton module. Some titles did. Some never will.
- **Vanguard** (Riot) and similar "we live in the kernel before the desktop does" systems are a hard no on Linux desktops.
- **User-space** anti-cheat (many older titles) often just works through Proton.

This list moves. Check:

1. ProtonDB reports dated this year, not 2021
2. The publisher's own Linux/Steam Deck post
3. AreWeAntiCheatYet

If you are buying a $70 seasonal shooter specifically to play ranked with friends on Tuesday night, verify *before* the refund window closes.

## What "works" means

A game can:

- Launch, then get kicked at the multiplayer menu
- Work on Steam Deck (a SteamOS environment) and still fail on your distro
- Work with Proton Experimental and fail on Proton 8

Deck verification is a hint, not a guarantee, because Valve's OS is a known target and your Arch box is not.

## Practical strategy

Keep a small Windows partition or another PC for the two games that refuse. Daily-drive Linux anyway. This is not surrender; it is how a lot of very online Linux gamers actually live.

Single-player, co-op PvE, and native titles are the abundant case. Let them be the abundant case.

## If you remember one thing

Anti-cheat is politics and kernel modules, not a Proton skill issue. Look it up per game. Do not take a Twitter screenshot from 2023 as the last word.
